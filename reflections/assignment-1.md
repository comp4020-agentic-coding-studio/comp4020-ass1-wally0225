# Reflection

## Project Introduction

For Assignment 1, I created an interactive explainer about how energy moves through a food chain. I wanted to focus on something that people encounter indirectly in everyday life but may not have a clear mental model of: what actually happens to energy as it moves from one organism to another.

The core idea of the project is simple: as you move up the food chain, the animals can become larger and more powerful, but the amount of available energy becomes smaller. I represented this through a simplified marine food chain, beginning with the Sun and phytoplankton and moving through zooplankton, krill, small fish, mackerel, squid, tuna, seal, great white shark, and finally orca.

Rather than presenting the food chain as a traditional diagram or a collection of information cards, I made scrolling the main interaction. As the visitor scrolls down, the page moves horizontally through the food chain. Each stage introduces the next organism and shows the predator-prey relationship, while an energy indicator gradually decreases. This means that the interaction itself becomes the explanation: the visitor does not simply read that energy is lost between trophic levels, but watches the available energy shrink as they progress through the chain.

I deliberately kept the scope narrow. Instead of trying to explain the entire ecosystem, different types of food webs, or detailed information about each animal, I focused on one question: where does the energy go as we move up the food chain? The visual progression from smaller organisms to larger predators provides the narrative, while the decreasing energy provides the underlying scientific concept I want the visitor to notice.

## What was the breakthrough that moved the work forward?

One of the biggest challenges in this project was deciding how long the food chain should be. I initially wanted to include around 20 organisms because I thought a longer chain would create more opportunities for interaction and make the experience more interesting. However, as I discussed the idea with AI and investigated the relationships between different organisms, I realised that a long, perfectly linear food chain does not accurately represent how ecosystems work. In reality, food webs are much more interconnected, and many predators have multiple prey.

I spent quite a lot of time struggling with this problem. I considered using different levels of predators and simply showing animals becoming larger, even when there was no direct relationship between them. However, this felt visually interesting but scientifically misleading. The breakthrough came when I stopped trying to maximise the number of organisms and instead prioritised having a food chain that was both reasonably credible and long enough to support the interaction I wanted.

I eventually chose a simplified marine food chain, moving from phytoplankton through different levels of marine organisms and eventually to larger predators. This gave me enough stages to create a meaningful scrolling journey while still allowing me to explain one focused idea: as energy moves up the food chain, only a fraction of it is transferred to the next trophic level. Making this decision helped me understand that reducing scope can actually make an interactive explanation stronger, because every element has a clearer purpose.

## What did this work change about who I want to be as a software developer?

This project changed how I think about communication when working with AI. One of the most time-consuming parts of the process was creating the images for the organisms. I often had to communicate with AI more than ten times for a single image because it would repeatedly produce something that was close to what I wanted but not quite right.

I learned that when an AI gets stuck in a loop, simply asking it to "try again" is usually not enough. Providing a visual reference and giving precise, measurable instructions worked much better. For example, instead of saying that a shark's tail looked wrong, I could specify that I wanted the tail to be approximately 1.5 times larger and moved around 20 pixels lower. This made my intention much easier for the AI to understand and act on.

This changed how I want to work as a product manager. I realised that a product manager needs to be able to translate an idea or vision into clear requirements, constraints, examples, and actionable feedback that other people can actually work with. Whether I am communicating with designers, developers, stakeholders, or AI tools, having a clear mental model is not enough if I cannot communicate it effectively. In future projects, I want to become a product manager who can bridge the gap between an idea and its implementation by giving clear direction while still allowing the people I work with to contribute their own expertise. This experience showed me that effective communication is not just a soft skill; it is an essential part of turning a product vision into something real.
